Credit Default Swap

Insurance on someone else's debt

You pay a small premium every quarter. If the company it's written on actually defaults, you get paid a large sum. If it doesn't, you've just paid for insurance.

1

What it actually is

A CDS is insurance on debt. The protection buyer pays the seller a regular premium (the "spread"); in exchange, if the reference entity — the company or country the contract is written on — has a credit event, the seller pays the buyer for the loss.

"Credit event" usually means default, but can also cover a restructuring or a missed payment, depending on how the contract is written.

2

Why someone would do this

A bank holding a company's bonds can buy CDS on that company instead of selling the bonds outright — it keeps the position but removes the default risk.

Others buy CDS with no underlying bond at all, purely as a bet that a company's credit is about to deteriorate — or sell it purely to collect premium from a company they think is safe.

3

Who's actually doing this

Banks hedging loan books, bond investors managing credit risk without trading the bond itself, and hedge funds taking outright views on which companies — or countries — are headed for trouble.

CDS spreads are watched closely because they tend to move faster than credit ratings do.

Premium: paid quarterly (bps/yr) Payout: only on a credit event Reference: a specific issuer's debt Can be bought with no underlying bond
